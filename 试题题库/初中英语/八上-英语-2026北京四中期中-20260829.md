#### A Matter of Taste

Have you ever tried the jelly bean test? It's a fun experiment that shows how much we depend on our nose to taste. Just hold your nose closed, put a jelly bean in your mouth, and chew. You will mostly taste a plain sweetness. But the moment you let go of your nose, you'll get a powerful wave of fruit flavor (口味). This proves that what we call "taste" is really a team effort between our tongue and our nose.

When you chew, the food lets out tiny molecules (分子). On your tongue, tiny bumps hold your taste buds (味蕾). Inside these buds are even smaller parts called receptors (接受器). Think of them like puzzle pieces. When a sweet food molecule comes along, its unique shape fits perfectly into a sweet receptor, like the last piece of a jigsaw puzzle. The same is true for a bitter molecule—it has a different shape that only fits into a bitter receptor. When the molecule locks into its match, it sends a signal to your brain that says, "Sweet!" or "Bitter!"

However, for salty and sour tastes, the process is a little different; it's not shape-based. These molecules still connect to receptors on the tongue, which send a signal to the brain, letting it know something is salty or sour.

Meanwhile, smell molecules travel from the back of your mouth up into your nose. The flavor you experience comes from your taste buds and your nose working together. These are your body's tools to sense chemicals in food and the air. Your brain mixes the messages to finally figure out the flavor, letting you know if you're eating a strawberry or a lemon.

You might have seen a "tongue map" in a book, showing that we taste sweet only on the tip and bitter only at the back. The truth is that taste buds on all parts of the tongue have receptors for all tastes. You can test this by dipping a cotton swab (棉签) in saltwater or lemon juice or a sugar solution and touching the front, sides, and back of your tongue with it.

Our sense of taste is built—in for a reason. Babies are born loving sweet tastes, which tell our bodies that a food, like ripe fruit, is a good source of energy. On the other hand, they naturally dislike bitter tastes, which can act as a warning for poisons (有毒食物) in some plants. So, the amazing system of taste and smell doesn't just help us enjoy our food—it also helps keep us safe.

Today, researchers are using this knowledge to develop new kinds of fruits. They produce more of the natural scent molecules that make food taste sweeter, without adding extra sugar. This exciting work could soon give us peaches and tomatoes that taste incredibly sweet, leading to more delicious and healthier choices in the future.

You hit the alarm and sink back into bed. You get lost in social media until you realize hours have gone by. You treat yourself to your favorite junk food or stay up all night watching the newest TV series. For a moment, it feels good. But later? You're behind, still stressed, and a little disappointed in yourself.

We've all been there. And here's the problem: what we often call self-care is nothing more than self-comfort. It comforts you in the moment but leaves you worn out tomorrow—further from your goals and less confident in yourself. Self-care and self-comfort can share some similarities, but they have key underlying differences.

Self—comfort is a form of avoidance. It's what we do to take our minds off uncomfortable emotions or situations. Maybe you're avoiding studying for an exam or applying for a new job, so you reach for your phone and watch short videos one after another. While it provides short-term ease, it often fails to address the root causes of stress and can even lead to habits that do harm to our well-being.

In comparison, self-care means thoughtful actions that improve your physical, mental, and emotional well-being. You can ask yourself, "how do I feel after doing these?" If you feel relaxed and uplifted afterward, that's self-care. But if you find yourself mindlessly playing with your phone for hours, and feeling burnt out or regretful afterward, that's not self-care, but self-comfort. True self-care leaves you feeling recharged, cared for, and stronger.

Telling the difference between self—comfort and self—care can sometimes be tricky. For example, staying busy can feel rewarding at first because crossing tasks off your to-do list gives you a quick sense of satisfaction. However, if you're doing it to sidestep deeper emotions, it's just another form of self-comfort. When my daughter got an uncommon disease at age 8, I threw myself into work, thinking it would protect me from the flood of pain. But it took a real cost on me—stress, sleeplessness, and headaches. In this case, doing something "useful" was just another form of avoidance.

Self-care isn't a quick fix, but it's a lasting one. Think of it as a long-term investment (投资) in your health, wellbeing, energy, and mood. You might feel more tired or upset in the short term, but over time, you will start to gain a sense of achievement. Mindfully choosing true self-care over self-comfort isn't always easy. It takes effort, but every time you choose self-care, you take a step closer to a life filled with inner strength and peace.
